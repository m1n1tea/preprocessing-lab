# Controlled rolling of steel plate and strip

## T. Tanaka

The major purpose of controlled rolling is to refine grain structure and, thereby, to enhance both the strength and toughness of steel in the as-hot-rolled condition. If a survey is made of the development of controlled rolling, it can be seen that controlled rolling consists of three stages: (a) deformation in the recrystallization region at high temperatures; (b) deformation in the non-recrystallization region within a low temperature range above $A r _ { 3 } ;$ and (c) deformation in the austenite—ferrite region. It is stressed that the importance of deformation in the non-recrystallization region is in dividing an austenite grain into several blocks by the introduction of deformation bands within it. Deformation in the austenite—ferrite region gives a mixed structure consisting of equiaxed grains and subgrains after transformation and, thereby, it increases further the strength and toughness. The fundamental difference between conventionally hot-rolled and controlled-rolled steels lies in the fact that the nucleation of ferrite occurs exclusively at austenite grain boundaries in the former, while it occurs in the grain interior as well as at grain boundaries in the latter, leading to a more refined grain structure. In controlled-rolled steel a crystallographic texture develops, which causes planar anisotropies in mechanical properties and embrittlement in the through-thickness direction. The latter is shown to be the main cause of the delamination which appeared in the frac tured Charpy specimens. Fundamental aspects of controlled rolling, such as the recrystallization behaviour of austenite, the retardation mechanism of austenite recrystallization due to niobium, microstructural changes accompanying deformation, factors governing strength and toughness, etc., are reviewed. The practice of controlled rolling in plate and strip mills is outlined. IMR/79

## HISTORICAL SURVEY

Controlled rolling is a means of improving the properties of steel in the hot-rolled condition to a level equivalent to, or greater than those of highly alloyed or heat-treated steels. Modern controlled rolling has been practised on numerous mill and laboratory experiments which have been performed to obtain high strength, good toughness, and good weldability. The basic idea is to control the rolling process in order to refine the austenite structure, and thereby to give fine ferrite grains.

In 1924 Arrowsmith1 had already pointed out the advantage of grain refinement. It has been known for many years that improvements in mechanical properties result when structural steels are rolled to low finishing temperatures.2 Hanemann and Lucke³ interpreted this in terms of recrystallization after hot deformation with the α-grain size being reduced with decreasing deformation temperature in the γ-region. In 1947 Barr and Tipper4 showed that in mild steel plates the ductile-to-brittle transition temperature was raised by an increase in α-grain size. Examining as-rolled plates, Mackenzie⁵ showed a correlation between transition temperature and finish-rolling temperature, a factor affecting γ-grain size. Similar results have been reported by other investigators.6-11 The above reports have one factor in common: hot rolling was carried out as usual at high temperatures, and only the last one or two passes were rolled at a lower temperature. Therefore, the results obtained were not always definite, and were in disagreement with each other.

After World War 2, in order to prevent the occurrence of brittle fracture, notch toughness was required in shipbuilding and in other structural steel plates. In general, the refinements of microstructure by Al killing, normalizing, and an increase in the ratio of manganese to carbon content were adopted to meet the requirement for notch toughness. Instead of normalizing, however, controlled low-temperature hot rolling was being practised by some mills in Europe to provide increased notch toughness in hot-rolled plate.12 The practice involved making the last few hotrolling passes at temperatures lower than normal. The optimum temperature for the last pass was just above the Ar3 temperature. This rolling schedule was fairly similar to the present controlled rolling and its prototype.

In 1959, Beiser13 reported that the small addition of Nb caused a large increase in strength but reduced notch toughness markedly in as-hotrolled steel plates. It was Great Lakes Steel14 which produced as-hot-rolled high-strength Nbbearing steels for the first time in 1960. In order to prevent a deterioration in notch toughness, the application of controlled rolling to Nb-bearing steel was studied. According to Norén's review15:

(i) when hot rolled under conventional rolling conditions, Nb steel deteriorated markedly in toughness; (ii) when finish rolled at lower temperatures, the improvement in toughness was much greater for Nb steel than for Nb-free steel; and (iii) when finish rolled below $\mathbf { 8 0 0 ^ { \circ } C _ { \circ } }$ or when total reduction below $\mathbf { 9 0 0 ^ { \circ } C }$ was more than $3 0 \% ,$ notch toughness improved remarkably. The strength increase resulting from the addition of Nb was found to be due to precipitation hardening of fine Nb(C, N) which formed in the α-matrix during cooling.16 In order to make full use of precipitation hardening, Nb(C, N) had to be dissolved at a high temperature before hot rolling, which coarsened the γ-grain structure. When coarse γ was hot rolled conventionally, the transformed structure was a mixture consisting of coarse α and bainite, thereby deteriorating notch toughness. Using these methods, the basis of combining controlled rolling with Nb steel was established.

Research on controlled rolling was carried out by the BISRA group in the 1960s, which opened the way for the development of the present controlledrolling technology. It is difficult to pinpoint the beginning, but the development of $\mathrm{C}-\mathrm{Mn}-\mathrm{Nb}$ finegrained steel commenced about 1958.17 Duckworth et $a l . ^ { 1 8 }$ showed that with the C content below 0.1%, and by using Nb additions and a low finishing temperature with heavy draughting, very attractive properties could be obtained in the as-rolled condition. Pearlite-free steel19and pearlite-reduced steel18 proposed by Duckworth et al. were intended to improve weldability and toughness by reducing the C content, to increase strength by additions of Nb and/or V, and to improve toughness by using a low finishing temperature. It was Phillips and Chapman,20 Irani et $a l . . . ^ { 2 1 }$ and Jones and Rothwell22who, for the first time, turned their attention to controlled rolling itself. They showed that Nb retarded recrystallization greatly, thereby enabling the deformation of γ in the non-recrystallization region, that transformation from deformed γ produced a fine α-grain structure, and that an improper draught schedule tended to cause a mixed grain structure. However, because their experiments were carried out in a laboratory bar mill, and the only measure of controlled rolling was the finishing temperature, their results lacked generality.

Research on controlled rolling motivated by the BISRA group has been undertaken very actively, including fundamental and applied aspects. Fundamental aspects include items such as the recrystalization behaviour of $\gamma , ^ { 2 3 - 2 9 }$ the retardation mechanism of recrystallization due to $\mathrm{Nb},^{22,30,31}$ microstructural change accompanying deformation in the non-recrystallization γ-region²7,32-34and its influence on transformation behaviour,34,35 and factors governing grain structure after transformation.33,34

Through these investigations, the importance of deformation in the non-recrystallization region was recognized. It was also made clear that controlled rolling was not to cease in the γ-region, but was to be extended into the γ—α-region, thereby enhancing both strength and toughness.27,36,37 Modern controlled rolling means the overall technology consisting of (i) a suitable selection of chemical composition, (ii) a proper slab-reheating temperature to obtain small and uniform γ-grains, (iii) γ-grain refinement owing to repeated deformation recrystallization, (iv) deformation in the non-recrystallization region, (v) deformation in the γ—α two-phase region, and (vi) controlled cooling.

The full-scale production of controlled-rolled plates and hot-rolled strip commenced with the construction of large-diameter pipelines, which required high-strength steel with good toughness and weldability. In fact, pipelines applied to Arctic-temperature service were impossible to construct without controlled-rolled plates.

Finally, the great contribution from Hall and Petch must be recognized. The Hall-Petch relation8 correlates yield stress with ferrite grain size. Heslop and Petch9 also associated transition temperature with grain size; they guided the development of controlled rolling throughout its whole course.

## FUNDAMENTALS OF CONTROLLED ROLLING

The major purpose of controlled rolling is to refine the structure of steel and, thereby, to enhance both strength and toughness. Because of the relation between γ- and α-grain sizes, refinement of the α-structure is achieved mainly through γ- grain refinement. The change in grain structure with deformation temperature has been studied by many investigators.25,27,33,37-43 Many studies were confined to deformation in the γ-region alone,25,33,38-41 while few of them were extended to deformation in the γ—α two-phase region.27,37,42,43

Kozasu et al. 25 divided deformation temperatures in the γ-region into three ranges in association with the change in γ- and α-grain structures. Deformation above $\mathbf { 1 0 0 0 ^ { \circ } C }$ brings about coarse, recrystallized γ-grains, which transform to a relatively coarse α and upper bainitic structure (range 1). Deformation in the intermediate temperature range from 1000° to 900°C refines γ by repeated recrystallization, leading to fine-grained α (range 2). Deformation below the recrystallization temperature produces 'warm-worked'γ, which leads to a finer α-grain structure (range 3). The grain refinement is achieved mainly by deformation in ranges 2 and 3.40 The transition temperature was shown to decrease in a linear manner with an increase in total reduction in ranges 2 and 3.40

However, when the recrystallized grain size is small, upper bainite is not allowed to form, even with moderate deformation, giving fine-grained α. Sekine and Maruyama4i emphasized that the best way is to refine γ through recrystallization as much as possible (type 1B), followed by deformation in the non-recrystallization region (type 2) and whereby a uniform, fine-grained α-structure is produced.

The difference between Fukuda et al.33 and Sekine and Maruyama41 is that the former stressed type 2 to achieve a fine-grained α-structure, whereas the latter emphasized the importance of the combination of types 1B and 2. This argument is, however, reduced to the uniformity of α nucleation within unrecrystallized γ-grains which, in turn, is reduced to the uniformity in deformationband density throughout unrecrystallized grains, since α nucleates on deformation bands as well as at γ-grain boundaries. There has been some evidence that deformation-band density has the propensity to be heterogeneous and that not all the deformation bands have the same ferrite-nucleating potential,34,41 suggesting that type 2 tends to produce a mixed α-grain structure compared with the optimum combination of types 1B and 2.

So far, controlled rolling has been confined to deformation in the γ-region. Coleman et $a l . ^ { 3 7 }$ and Gladman and Dulieu42obtained a further increase in strength by extending deformation into the γ + α and/or α-regions, and by developing a substructure. Advancing this idea, Tanaka et al.27,45 proposed the three stages of the controlled-rolling process: (i) deformation in the γ-recrystallization region, (ii) deformation in the non-recrystallization region, and (iii) deformation in the γ—α two-phase region.

Grain refinement through repeated recrystallization does not proceed indefinitely but reaches a certain limiting value b and thereby results in relatively coarse α-grains $b ^ { \prime }$ . A means of breaking the limiting value is to divide the γ-grain. Since a recrystallized $\gamma -$ grain b is divided by deformation bands $c ,$ the final α-grain structure is much smaller for $c ^ { \prime }$ than for $b ^ { \prime }$ . The deformation in the two-phase region causes further grain refinement and a mixed structure consisting of equiaxed grains and subgrains. Additional strengthening is obtained by subgrain hardening.

The fundamental difference between conventional hot rolling and controlled rolling lies in that, in the former, α nucleates exclusively at γ-grain boundaries, whereas in the latter α nucleation occurs at grain interiors as well as grain boundaries, which leads to a large difference in the final α-grain structure between the two. The fact that the deformation band is equivalent to the γ-grain boundary with regard to ferrite-nucleating potentiality, means that a γ-grain is divided into several blocks by deformation bands. A comparison between conventional hot rolling and controlled rolling is very similar to that between normalizing and quenching. In conventionally hot-rolled and normalized steeis, α nucleates exclusively at γ-grain boundaries, which limits grain refinement, whereas in controlled-rolled and quenched steels, the $\gamma -$ grain is divided into several blocks; the former is by deformation bands and the latter by martensitic transformation, producing a much finer grain structure.43

Since unrecrystallized $\gamma$ containing deformation bands promotes the γ to α transformation, from a different viewpoint, controlled rolling can be defined as a process for accelerating α transformation and thereby for producing fine-grained α in steel with medium hardenability which, without being controlled rolled, will transform to a bainitic structure, giving poor toughness.

## Static recrystallization

Static recrystallization accompanying hot deformation proceeds in a similar manner to that in coldworked material. Nucleation sites for new grains are predominantly triple junctions of grains and grain boundaries, and nucleation within grains is almost nil.25 The distribution of nuclei is highly localized and inhomogeneous; while some grain boundaries give rise to numerous nuclei, there are boundaries where nuclei are completely absent.25 The progress of recrystallization after nucleation is essentially the migration of the recrystallizing front into deformed matrix as is usual in recrystallization. The completion of recrystallization is followed by normal grain growth.

Effects of the amount of deformation and deformation temperature on recrystallized grain size are shown in Fig.7. The recrystallized grain size decreases rapidly as the amount of reduction increases, reaching a limiting value.27 The limiting grain size decreases with reducing initial grain size.49Although a lower deformation temperature produces smaller grain sizes, the effect of temperature is slight. On the contrary, it is necessary to apply more deformation than that required for recrystallization. The critical amount of deformation, $R _ { \mathbf { c r i t } } ,$ required for the completion of recrystallization is small, as is the temperature dependence in plain C steel. In Nb steel, the effect of grain size on $R _ { \mathbf { c r i t } }$ is extremely large, and $R _ { \mathbf { c r i t } }$ increases rapidly with decreasing deformation temperature. Recrystallization is virtually suppressed below 950°C.

Ouchi et al.51 and Tanaka et al.28,29,52 studied the general recrystallization behaviour by single-pass rolling, shown in Fig.10. Recrystallization behaviour is divided into three regions, recovery, partial recrystallization, and recrystallization. The recrystallization region is subdivided into static and dynamic. The critical amount of deformation dividing each region increases rapidly with decreasing deformation temperature. It is also influenced markedly by the addition of microalloying elements, and in particular Nb.51,52 Reduction in the recrystallization region produces a fine, uniform recrystallized grain structure. Reduction in the partial-recrystal lization region produces a mixed structure consisting of recrystallized grains and recovered grains. Reduction in the recovery region produces locally very coarse grains due to strain-induced grain-boundary migration, while most grains remain unchanged, releasing stored energy by recovery,28,52 As is clear from Fig. 10, it is very difficult to roll steel in the recrystallization region and, in particular, during the initial passes in the plate mill, since $R _ { \mathbf { c r i t } }$ is extremely large. Tanaka et al. 28,52 described clearly how to achieve grain refinement and how to obtain a uniform grain structure under these conditions. Even if deformation is applied repeatedly in the recovery region, very sluggish and incomplete recrystallization occurs, while huge grains larger than the initial size are produced locally and persist throughout rolling, see Fig. 11, which leads to the formation of a mixed α-grain structure. However, once huge grains are formed by a slight reduction in the recovery region, they persist even after many passes in the partial-recrystallization region. In summarizing the above description, if the initial rolling passes are made in the recovery region, huge grains are formed locally, owing to the straininduced grain-boundary migration, which persist throughout the whole course of rolling. ## $A r _ { 3 }$ temperature during controlled rolling

Depending on its purpose, controlled rolling is performed either in the γ-region alone or in the two-phase region. In controlled-rolled steel, strain energy stored during deformation in the non-recrystallization region accelerates the $\gamma$ to α transformation, i.e. a strain-induced transformation takes place, thereby raising the ${ A r } _ { 3 }$ temperature. The mechanism initiating separation may depend on chemical composition, microstructure, and hot-rolling condition. When stress is induced in the through-thickness direction, cracks initiate at the place where the fracture strength is lowest, and propagate along the second weakest location. When flattened MnS exists in steel, it triggers the initiation of the crack, which propagates along grains with {100} orientation and/or bainite and/or the pearlite band, depending on the situation. If MnS exists very occasionally in steel with an extremely low S content, separation will initiate at the second weakest location.

## Elimination of separation

Various methods have been proposed to prevent the occurrence of separations.27,110-112 In order to eliminate flattened MnS which triggers the initiation of separation, inclusion-shape control has been employed, which converts elongated sulphide to a globular inclusion by the addition of rare earth metals111,113 and/or Ca.112,114 Advancing the sulphide-shape control·technique, a new desulphurization method has been developed, which can reduce the S content to an extremely low level. Finish rolling in the γ-region results in the formation of a weak {100} texture, suppressing markedly the occurrence of separation. A decrease in the P content is desirable, since it causes temper embrittlement during slow cooling after coiling in hot-rolled strip.108 Once initiated separation has a propensity to propagate along the banded structure.110 Mori et al.110 reported that the occurrence of separation is determined by the combined effect of band density and {100} intensity, and that the addition of Cr disperses a banded structure, thereby suppressing the occurrence of separations.

## Interpretation of mechanical properties

The reports on variation in transition temperature with deformation temperature in the two-phase region have been in disagreement with each other. As shown in Fig. 33, the transition temperature increased with a decrease in finishing temperature in the two-phase region; it reached a minimum at a certain temperature in the two-phase region, and increased again with a further decrease in finishing temperature.20,44,78 The transition temperature decreased with decreasing deformation temperature78: excessive reduction in the twophase region raised the transition temperature $_ { 1 } 2 7 ;$ and heavy reduction in the two-phase region lowered the transition temperature of plain carbon steel.79,80 Those contradictory reports are explained consistently by equation (14). In steel deformed in the two-phase region, the second term $A ( \sigma _ { \mathrm { p h } } + \sigma _ { \mathrm { d h } } + \Delta )$ and the last term $E n _ { \mathbf { S } }$ on the right-hand side of equation (14) have a particularly large influence on transition temperature. If the contribution to the transition temperature from the second term, in particular dislocation hardening $\sigma _ { \mathbf { d h } } ,$ is larger than that from separation, the transition temperature increases. On the other hand, if the contribution from separation is larger than that from dislocation hardening, the transition temperature decreases.

When deformed just below the $A r _ { 3 }$ temperature, the cellular dislocation structure changes quickly into a subgrain structure, eliminating large dislocation hardening. However, when deformed at lower temperatures, insufficient recovery raises $\sigma _ { \mathsf { d h } } ,$ which exceeds the effect of separation and thereby increases the transition temperature. In Nb steel, conflict between dislocation hardening and separation is severe because fine precipitates of strain-induced Nb(C, N) pin down dislocations, and thereby retard recovery and recrystallization. On the other hand, in C-Mn steel, dislocation hardening is small owing to the rapid progress of recovery and, at the same time, there is a large effect of separation due to the development of {100} texture, which causes a decrease in transition temperature even when finish rolled at low temperatures and/or heavily deformed.

## EFFECT OF ALLOYING ELEMENTS

Alloying elements which enhance strength and toughness are most desirable. There are three ways of increasing strength; grain refining, pre-cipitation and/or solid-solution hardening, and transformation hardening. An improvement in toughness is achieved by either grain refining or by toughening the α-matrix itself. Since grain refining enhances both strength and toughness, grain-refining elements are most desirable. Grain refinement is attained by fine dispersion of a second phase and/or by lowering the transformation temperature by the addition of substitutional alloying elements. Needless to say the purpose of controlled rolling is to achieve grain refinement and thereby to improve mechanical properties. The effect of each alloying element is represented in terms of its contribution to strength and transition temperature.120-122 Besides strength and toughness, weldability is a very important factor, which improves with the decreasing carbon equivalent and, in particular, C content.

The microalloying elements, such as Nb and Ti, are indispensable in controlled-rolled steel. In the early stage of development, Nb was employed mainly for precipitation hardening. However, since Nb was found to improve strength and toughness very effectively through grain refinement, it has become an indispensable element in controlledrolled steel. The effects of Nb, V, and Ti on strength are shown in Fig. 34. Nb contributes to strength via both grain refining and precipitation; particularly in a low Nb content, major hardening is due to grain refinement. V causes a large increase in both strength and transition temperature through precipitation hardening. Ti causes a slight increase in strength via grain refinement and pre-cipitation hardening. Therefore, only Nb enhances both strength and toughness in controlled-rolled steel. The main cause of grain refinement in Nb steel is that Nb in solution delays γ-recrystallization, thereby raising the recrystallization temperature, extending the non-recrystallization region, and intensifying the effect of controlled rolling. It is also considered that strain-induced precipitates of fine Nb(C, N) prevent α-grain growth during the γ to α transformation.123

## PRACTICE OF CONTROLLED ROLLING IN PRODUCTION MILL

In the production mill, factors governing the controlled-rolling process are inter-related and a variety of approaches can be used to attain the same objective. The important factors are as follows: (i) lowering of the slab-reheating temperature to obtain small and uniform γ-grain size29; (ii) γ-grain refinement via recrystallization; (iii) selection of a suitable pass schedule and, in particular, the amount of reduction per pass during the initial passes to obtain a fine and uniform recrystallized γ-grain structure; (iv) selection of temperature and time of delay between the recrystallization region and the non-recrystallization region22,36,39; and (v) a suitable amount of reduction and rolling temperature in the two-phase region. They are virtually equivalent to and interchangeable with each other, implying that a suitable combination of some of them would suffice to control-roll steel adequately. It is for this reason that the stressing factor differs from one investigator to another and from one mill to another. Taking into consideration the capacity, efficiency, cooling power, and stability of operation, each mill has its own controlled-rolling process.

The plate mill has difficulty in giving a sufficient amount of reduction per pass and, in particular, during the initial several passes of slab rolling. An improper pass schedule leads to mixed grain structure, causing deterioration in toughness. In order to overcome this difficulty, some investigators emphasize extremely large deformation in the non-recrystallization region.33,44 On the other hand, owing to a great deal of freedom in selecting the rolling temperature and distribution of reduction among the recrystallization, non-recrystallization, and twophase regions, the plate mill can meet freely a variety of requirements. In the hot-strip mill, the reduction per pass can be more than the critical one required for the completion of recrystallization in each pass, which makes reduction very easy in the recrystallization region, producing a finer and more uniform γ-grain structure than the plate mill. In the strip mill, however, the time and temperature of holding, distribution of reduction among the recrystallization, non-recrystallization, and two-phase regions, and selection of the finishing temperature are quite restricted. Thus, the plate mill and hot-strip mill have their advantages and disadvantages. The practice of controlled rolling is described briefly below.

As shown in Fig. 1, controlled rolling in the plate mill is often carried out in terms of a 'rough-hold-finish'schedule: rolling passes in the rougher in the rapid-recrystallization region, a delay period, and rolling passes in the finisher. A delay is necessary to allow a specified total deformation to be introduced in the non-recrystallization region. There is a great possibility that during the holding period, recrystallization and subsequent rapid grain growth will take place22,39,129or partial recrystallization will occur38,39 whereby a mixed α-grain structure is formed, deteriorating toughness. Care must be taken, therefore, to ensure the required low finishing temperature while pre-venting rapid grain growth or partial recrystallization during holding.

Instead of the rough—holdfinish schedule, Jones and Rothwell22adopted a 'continuous' schedule involving reduction in the whole range, which produces a uniform, fine-grained structure. Figure 35 depicts the new rolling schedule,i.e.the three-stage rolling process as well as the standard two-stage process (rough—hold—finish), employed by USINOR, Dunkirk.129 During the delay time at high temperature in the standard two-stage process, recrystallization is rapid even in Nb steel, leading to a coarse-grained structure at the end of the delay time. In the three-stage process, the first delay time at high temperature leads to a coarse-grained structure. During the second delay time, recrystallization is sluggish so that the grain size at the end of rolling is finer for the threestage process than for the second stage.

At the British Steel Corporation, General Steels Division,36 to minimize recrystallization and grain growth during the delay period, the temperature at the commencement of the delay period is reduced by reducing the reheating temperature.

The temperature at the end of the delay period is controlled in order to allow a specific total deformation to be introduced without inducing an excessively high mill loading. Finish rolling at temperatures in the two-phase and/or α-regions is employed to increase strength. Total rolling deformation in the non-recrystallization region after the delay period is very important in order to achieve a uniform, fine-grained structure. To fulfil this requirement, the thickness of material at the commencement of re-rolling after the delay period is controlled at either three times or five times that of the final thickness.130-132

Controlled rolling in the hot-strip mill is very different from that in the plate mill: (i) rapid cooling on the run-out table after finish rolling is followed by slow cooling after coiling; (ii) both the rougher and finisher can carry out heavy reduction — during rolling in the finisher, the interpass time is very short, total rolling deformation in the finisher becoming almost one-pass reduction; (iii) rolling temperature and total reduction in the finisher encounter much restriction; (iv) rougher rolling can be regarded as deformation in the recrystallization region, and finisher rolling as deformation in the non-recrystallization region — the total reduction is overwhelmingly larger for rougher rolling than for finisher rolling and reduction per pass is large, which is suitable for achieving fine-grained γ through repeated recrystallization, resulting in a uniform, fine-grained α-structure; and (v) during holding at the coiling temperature, precipitation of Nb and V proceeds causing a large increase in yield strength. Rolling in the two-phase region is not always carried out, causing few separations. Because of large pre-cipitation hardening and few separations, the transition temperature is usually much higher for hotrolled strip than for plate. One example of a rolling schedule is shown in Fig. 36. By employing a low slab-reheating temperature and a heavy reduction in the rougher, they obtained a uniform, finegrained structure, even though the total reduction in the finisher (in the non-recrystallization region) was about 50%.

## ENLARGEMENT OF CONTROLLED-ROLLING PROCESS

As controlled rolling is known to be very useful, it has been applied to various fields and its application is being expanded.

## Acicular ferrite steel

Acicular ferrite, HSLA steel has been developed by the Climax Molybdenum Company.127,128,134 Lowcarbon (≤0.06%) Mn—Mo—Nb steel which will transform to upper bainite from undeformed or conventionally hot-rolled γ, transforms to acicular ferrite, when transformation is accelerated by controlled rolling. Figure 37 shows the acicular ferrite structure (Fig.37a) as well as the bainitic structure (Fig.37b). The acicular ferrite structure consists of fine non-equiaxed ferrite dispersed with cementite and martensite islands.

The acicular ferrite is defined as a highly substructured, non-equiaxed phase that forms on continuous cooling by a mixed diffusion and shear mode of transformation at a temperature slightly higher than the upper bainite transformation range.127 Acicular ferrite is different from bainitic ferrite with respect to the prior γ-grainboundary network and cementite and martensite morphologies; in the bainitic structure, the prior γ-boundary network is retained and plate-like cementites are formed at coarse lath boundaries as well as the prior grain boundaries, whereas in the acicular ferrite structure the prior γ-grainboundary network is completely eliminated having a fine-grained structure, and small cementite and martensite islands are scattered throughout the matrix (see Fig. 37). Because of the above difference in microstructure between bainitic and acicular ferrite there is a distinct difference in the facet unit (effective grain size) which, in turn, causes a large difference in transition temperature between the two. In the bainitic structure, a facet unit is nearly equal to the prior γ-grain size, whereas in the acicular ferrite a facet unit is much smaller than the prior γ-grain size, giving a low transition temperature.

## As-hot-rolled dual-phase steel

Dual-phase steel has a microstructure consisting of α-grains dispersed with fine martensite islands. It is characterized by continuous yielding followed by rapid work hardening, which results in a low yield but high tensile strength and large elongation. Dual-phase steel can be produced in the as-hotrolled condition by the combination of suitable chemical composition and rolling conditions.137-141When steel containing both strong ferrite formers like Si and transformation-delaying elements such as Cr, Mn, and/or Mo is cooled after hot rolling, the microstructure consists of α- and γ-phases. The latter is stabilized due to carbon enrichment and thereby transforms to martensite at low temperatures. When deformation is applied in the non-recrystallization region,

strain-assisted γ to α transformation occurs, thereby raising the α-transformation temperature on one hand and reducing the martensite transformation temperature on the other, which makes it easy to produce ferrite plus martensite dualphase steel.

## Controlled rolling of C—Mn steel

Gohda et $a l . ^ { 7 9 }$ and Hashimoto et al.80reported that when deformed in the two-phase region, Nbfree steel exhibits improvements in strength and toughness and, further, that heavy deformation in the two-phase region does not deteriorate toughness. Increase in strength by deformation in the two-phase region is due to either subgrain formation in the recovery region or dislocation hardening in the non-recovery region.80 The ease of recovery and occurrence of separations are responsible for good toughness of steel obtained by heavy deformation in the two-phase region.80 The effect of deformation in the two-phase region is essentially the same between Nb-bearing and Nbfree steels. The difference lies in the ease of recovery, which causes differences in the optimum conditions of finishing temperature and/or strain. Controlled-rolled C—Mn steel has started to be used as material for low-temperature service.

## Controlled rolling as pretreatment

By making use of grain-refining effect, controlled rolling is employed as a pretreatment before normalizing or quenching and tempering. This is based on the fact that a fine-grained structure produced by controlled rolling persists even in the reheated condition, probably due to the dispersion of fine Nb(C, N) precipitates. In Nb and 3. 5%Ni steels, controlled rolling plus normalizing produces higher strengths and better toughness than normalizing alone.142,143

Linepipe steel for Arctic use is usually produced by controlled rolling. However, when the thickness is increased too much the controlledrolling effect cannot be exerted over the whole thickness. Controlled rolling plus quenching and tempering produces much tougher material than the single process alone. The fine γ-grain size before quenching is responsible for this.144 Controlled rolling plus controlled cooling and controlled rolling followed by direct quenching have been tried.145

A new type of thermomechanical treatment has been proposed by Miyoshi et al.146 After controlled rolling, C-Mn and Nb steels are cooled below the ${ A r } _ { 1 }$ temperature and subsequently heated to $\sim 9 \dot { 0 } 0 ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C }$ , which produces a very finegrained γ-structure owing to the superimposed effects of controlled rolling, transformation, and normalizing. Fine-grained γ is once again controlled rolled, producing an extremely fine and uniform α-grain structure.

## Continuum rolling

So far, controlled rolling in the γ—α two-phase and/ or α regions has been described.Melloy and Dennison78 proposed continuum rolling, a unique thermomechanical process involving reductions of γ, γ + α, and α. Figure 39 shows that with continuum rolling the yield strength increases, while the transition temperature decreases with a decrease in finishing temperature in the α-region. Melloy and Dennison78attributed this desirable combination of increased strength and toughness to (i) grain refinement of both γ and α by heavy, repeated deformations; (ii) a dynamic recovery that produces a fine polygonal substructure; and (iii) cube-on-corner crystallographic texturing that results from the deformation process.

The results were obtained in extra-low-carbon steel but whether or not the same results will be obtained in low-alloy steels remains unsolved. It is also difficult to apply this process for practical use.

## SUMMARY

A review has been made of the controlled rolling of steel, covering such aspects as its historical development, the fundamentals of controlled rolling, recrystallization behaviour in Nb steel, the formation and role of deformation bands, properties of controlled-rolled steels, effects of alloying elements, the practice of controlled rolling in production mills, and future developments.

Rolling passes in the low-temperature range just above $A r _ { 3 }$ were made to refine the grain structure and thereby to enhance both strength and toughness in as-rolled steel. Their influence was found to be very beneficial when applied to Nb steels. This was the beginning of controlled rolling.

Controlled rolling consists of three stages: (i) stage 1 is deformation in the recrystallization region, where coarse austenite is refined by recrystallization but still transforms to a relatively coarse ferrite; (ii) stage 2 is deformation in the non-recrystallization region, where deformation bands are formed in unrecrystallized austenite ferrite nucleates on the deformation bands as well as on the austenite grain boundaries, giving fine ferrite; and (iii) stage 3 is deformation in the austenite—ferrite two-phase region — deformation in this region continues stage 2 and also deforms the ferrite, producing a substructure. Additional strengthening is obtained by the subgrain hardening.

The fundamental difference between conventionally hot-rolled and controlled-rolled steels lies in that in the former ferrite nucleates exclusively at austenite grain boundaries, whereas in the latter ferrite nucleation occurs in the grain interiors as well as the grain boundaries. This brings about a large difference in the final ferrite grain size between the two.

Controlled-rolled steel has higher strength and a lower transition temperature than conventionally hot-rolled or normalized steel. In controlled-rolled steel, however, plane anisotropy in the mechanical properties and through-thickness embrittlement are caused by crystallographic texture development, manganese sulphide inclusions flattened by low-temperature rolling, and others. Through-thickness embrittlement is the main cause of the occurrence of separations which appear in the fractured Charpy specimens in the longitudinal and transverse directions. Very low transition temperatures in the longitudinal and transverse directions are achieved by sacrificing toughness in the through-thickness direction. Several procedures are proposed to eliminate separations.

Recrystallization behaviour is divided into the recovery, partial recrystallization, and complete recrystallization regions. To obtain uniform, finegrained austenite and ferrite structures, at least one rolling pass must be performed successively in a strain range larger than that for the partialrecrystallization region. The retardation mechanism of austenite recrystallization due to Nb is explained in terms of strain-induced precipitation of fine Nb(C, N) and/or solute-drag effect. Deformation bands generated by controlled rolling divide an austenite grain into several blocks, since they are equivalent to austenite grain boundaries

with regard to ferrite-nucleation potential. In controlled-rolled steels, the microstructure consists of fine-grained polygonal ferrite or a mixture of polygonal ferrite grains and subgrains, depending on whether deformation is finished in the austenite or austenite—ferrite region. Taking microstructural change into consideration, strengthening and toughening mechanisms are presented. Transition temperature is determined mainly by the following two contradictory factors; hardening due to precipitation, dislocations, etc., and grain refinement and separations.

Although there are many processing variables governing controlled rolling, some of them are virtually equivalent to and interchangeable with each other. It is for this reason that the stressing factor is different from one mill to another. As controlled rolling is known to be very useful, it has been applied to various fields and its application is being expanded. Acicular ferrite and ferrite plus martensite dual-phase steels are produced by the combination of suitable chemical composition and controlled rolling. By making use of the grainrefining effect, controlled rolling is employed as pretreatment before normalizing or quenching.
